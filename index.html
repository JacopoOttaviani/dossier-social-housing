The Homes Europe Stopped Building

Europe has around 18 million social homes, offering rents below market rates to those who need them most. They make up a third of all housing in the Netherlands, but just one home in forty in Italy. Over four decades, cities have sold off public housing and let subsidies dry up, leaving millions of households on waiting lists. This dossier examines what six cities reveal about the erosion — and the future — of public housing in Europe.

November 2026 · Data: OECD, Housing Europe, Eurostat, municipal registers · ~10 min read

Photo: Le Corbusier's Unité d'Habitation, Marseille — Claudia Salini / Unsplash

Most major European cities have waiting lists for social housing. In London, 341,421 households are on the list, the highest number since 2013. Paris has 327,000 applicants, while across France the total reached 2.8 million in 2024. In Amsterdam, the wait is measured in years: tenants can expect to wait around a decade for a home. Vienna is a notable exception. According to the city, around half its residents live in municipal or subsidised housing, and in 2025 it approved 4,054 new subsidised homes. Finland offers another model. Until 2024, it was the only European country where homelessness had declined over the long term. In Helsinki, the Housing First approach gives people experiencing homelessness a permanent home without requiring them to meet preconditions first.

Social housing consists of homes rented below market rates and allocated according to need, rather than to those willing or able to pay the most. The OECD, a group of mostly high-income countries, counts nearly 28 million such homes across its member states, around 18 million of them in Europe.

On average, social housing accounts for 8 per cent of the housing stock in the EU, but national figures vary widely. In the Netherlands, it makes up 34 per cent of all homes; in Austria, 24 per cent; in Denmark, 21 per cent; in England, 16 per cent; and in France, 14 per cent. Elsewhere, the share drops sharply: below 3 per cent in Germany, Hungary and Slovakia, 2.4 per cent in Italy, and around 1 per cent in Spain and Portugal. Greece has no social rental housing sector to speak of, following the closure of its workers' housing agency in 2012.

The trend is equally stark. According to the OECD's latest figures, the social housing stock shrank over the past decade in 18 of the 25 member countries with available data. In Europe, it declined in 15 of the 18 countries for which figures are available for both years.

Much of this decline was the result of political choices. Cities and governments sold off social housing, often offering tenants the chance to buy their homes at a discount, or allowed subsidies to expire.

Keeping social housing has not shielded cities from rising rents. But it has allowed many households to remain outside the private rental market. In Vienna, 42 per cent of households rent from the city or from limited-profit housing providers, which are legally required to reinvest their profits in housing. Researchers have described the stability of social housing in Vienna and Helsinki as "remarkable".

In cities that sold off their housing stock, public spending has not disappeared; instead, it has taken other forms. London boroughs are now buying back homes sold to tenants through the Right to Buy scheme. In Islington, some have cost more than twice the original sale price. Meanwhile, 76,020 London households live in temporary accommodation, including bed-and-breakfast hotels and hostels.

The European Union has only recently begun to tackle the housing crisis. In December 2025, the European Commission unveiled its first Affordable Housing Plan, under the EU's first commissioner for housing, backed by an investment platform at the European Investment Bank. On 9 September 2026, it proposed an Affordable Housing Act, now before the European Parliament and EU governments. The proposed law would establish common rules for local housing measures, including when cities can restrict short-term holiday rentals in areas facing housing shortages.

This dossier examines the starting point for these new measures. The experience of six cities points to a common lesson: where social housing has endured or expanded, it has been backed by long-term political commitment and lasting funding mechanisms, from a payroll levy in Vienna to a legal quota in Paris. In London and Berlin, meanwhile, authorities are now paying far more per home to buy back housing than they received when they sold it.

01 · Europe

From 34 per cent in the Netherlands to 2.4 in Italy

The five countries with the largest social housing sectors — the Netherlands, Austria, Denmark, the United Kingdom and France — all maintained substantial stocks through the 1990s and 2000s. In southern Europe and the former communist countries of eastern Europe, social housing was either never developed on a comparable scale or was sold off to tenants after 1990. Today, the share of social housing in the country with the highest proportion is more than 40 times that in the country with the lowest.

02 · Six cities

City by city

Vienna kept building. Helsinki used social housing to tackle homelessness, even as it became a smaller share of a growing city's housing stock. Paris increased its share under a national law requiring municipalities to provide more social housing. Berlin and London went in the opposite direction, losing much of their stock: Berlin through sales and expiring subsidies, London through sales. Rome still owns most of its social housing, but allocates very few homes. Each city profile below includes a map and key figures.

Vienna

Vienna never sold off its municipal flats, so it never had to buy them back. The city owns around 220,000 flats and describes itself as Europe's largest municipal landlord. Limited-profit housing associations, which are legally required to reinvest their profits in housing, own another 185,000 or so. Together, they house 42 per cent of the city's households.

Income limits are high enough that social housing is not reserved for the poorest: according to the city, three-quarters of Viennese residents earn below the threshold. Typically, 3,000 to 4,500 subsidised homes are completed each year. Approvals for new homes fell to around 2,000 in 2022, before rising to 4,054 in 2025.

Part of the funding comes from a levy on wages, set at 1 per cent in 1954. In January 2026, Vienna raised it to 1.5 per cent, becoming the only Austrian state to do so. The city's housing tradition goes back further still. Between 1923 and 1934, the socialist government known as Red Vienna built around 64,000 flats, financed in part by a special housing tax. That tax was abolished in the 1930s; today's wage levy is a separate measure, introduced later.

The rulebook · The city has owned housing since the 1920s (Red Vienna) · limited-profit landlords are regulated by a federal law, and some tenants can buy their flat · a housing levy on wages since 1952: 1% since 1954, 1.5% since January 2026 · the city does not sell its flats, and its subsidies do not expire.

Helsinki

Helsinki owns about 65 per cent of the land within its borders. By selling or leasing plots and setting targets for the mix of new housing, the city has considerable influence over what gets built, and for whom. Its housing company, Heka, is Finland's largest landlord, with around 55,000 flats. One in seven Helsinki residents lives in a Heka home.

Since 2008, these homes have been central to Finland's Housing First policy. A homeless person gets a permanent flat with no conditions attached, and help with other problems comes afterwards. For years, Finland was the only EU country where homelessness was consistently falling. The number of people living alone without a home dropped from around 17,100 in 1987 to 3,429 in 2023. The government elected that year did not abolish Housing First, but it weakened the safety net around it: cutting housing benefits and welfare payments, halving state funding for housing advice and reducing grants to charities that provide much of the support. Homelessness rose in 2024 for the first time since 2012, then increased by a further fifth in 2025.

The pressure is also visible in the shrinking share of subsidised housing. The number of such flats has remained broadly stable, at between 65,000 and 70,000, for two decades. But Helsinki's population has grown, so their share of the city's housing stock has fallen from 22 per cent in 2005 to 16 per cent. Some older flats have also gradually fallen outside the subsidy system.

The rulebook · The city owns about 65% of its land and leases much of it, with targets for the mix of new homes · cheap state-backed loans fund non-profit building · national Housing First programmes since 2008 · homeless shelters turned into flats · cuts to benefits, housing advice and charity funding from 2024.

Paris

Paris is the only city in this dossier to have substantially increased the share of its housing stock devoted to social housing. Much of that growth is the result of a legal obligation. A 2000 law, known as SRU, requires French municipalities above a certain size to ensure that social housing accounts for at least 20 per cent of their main homes. In 2013 the target was raised to 25 per cent. Towns that fall short pay fines. Since 2001 Paris has funded 130,316 social homes and raised its share from 13.4 to 23.2 per cent. Where it cannot build, it buys existing buildings: more than half of the homes funded since 2001 were bought, not built. Yet demand continues to outstrip supply. By the end of 2025, 327,000 households were on the waiting list, just under half of them already living in Paris. The gap between social and private rents helps explain the pressure. Private rents average €26.3 per square metre, roughly three times the average social rent. The problem extends well beyond the capital: in 2024, 2.8 million French households were waiting for social housing, more than in any other European country.

The rulebook · SRU law (2000): 20% social housing, raised to 25% in 2013; in 2022 the 2025 deadline was replaced by a rule that makes towns keep catching up · fines for towns that fall short · social rents capped nationally, depending on the area and how the home was funded · private rents capped again since July 2019.

Berlin

Berlin had 85,765 social-rent flats at the end of 2024, down from 370,861 in 1991. The decline has two main causes. The first is built into Germany's subsidy system: after 15 to 30 years, rent restrictions expire and social flats revert to the private market, even if their owners have not sold them. In 2025, 8,387 flats left the subsidy system, while the city approved funding for 5,175 new ones, most of which will take years to complete. The second cause was a wave of privatisations. Between 1990 and the mid-2000s, Berlin sold more than 200,000 publicly owned flats. The biggest deal came in 2004, when the city sold GSW, a housing company with 65,700 flats, to investment funds managed by Cerberus and Goldman Sachs. The price worked out at roughly €30,000 per flat, including the debt assumed by the buyers. The funds floated GSW on the stock market in 2011 and had sold their remaining shares by January 2012. In 2013 the company was taken over by Deutsche Wohnen. Berlin began reversing course in 2021, paying €2.46 billion, or around €167,000 per flat, to buy back 14,750 homes from Vonovia and Deutsche Wohnen. That September, 57.6 per cent of voters backed a proposal to bring the flats of the biggest private landlords into public ownership. According to the city government's estimate, it would affect around 243,000 homes. The referendum was not legally binding: it called on the government to draft a law, but did not oblige it to carry out the expropriation.

In March 2026, Berlin's parliament passed a law setting out a framework for such a takeover. But it will not take effect until 2028, and no flats have yet changed hands. In July 2026, the federal government announced plans to ban state laws that transfer rental housing into public ownership, putting the initiative's future in doubt.

The issue remains politically potent. On 20 September, Die Linke, the left-wing party that backed the 2021 campaign, won the largest share of the vote in Berlin's election, with 25.7 per cent.

The rulebook · Subsidies that expire after a set time (the German model) · city flats sold from 1990 to the mid-2000s, some since bought back · 2021 referendum on taking big landlords' flats into public ownership: passed, not yet carried out · law on how to do it passed March 2026, in force from 2028 · the city's own housing companies are adding homes again.

London

Introduced in 1980, Right to Buy allows council tenants to purchase their homes at a discount, initially of up to 50 per cent, later raised to 70 per cent. The scheme has permanently reduced London's stock of public housing. By March 2025, London's councils had sold 318,909 homes through Right to Buy — almost as many as the 341,421 households now on their waiting lists.

The number of council homes in London fell from a peak of 795,789 in 1986 to 389,819 in 2021. Not all disappeared into private ownership: some were transferred to housing associations, non-profit landlords that continue to let them as social housing. Even counting these homes, however, social housing's share of London's total housing stock fell from 29 per cent in 1991 to 21 per cent in 2025. Since 2021, the number of council homes has barely recovered, reaching just 394,591 in 2025.

The shortage has pushed more households into temporary accommodation, from B&Bs to hostels. By March 2026, 76,020 London households were living in such arrangements. London Councils, the association representing the capital's boroughs, estimates that they cost almost £5 million a day.

Some boroughs are buying back former council homes for more than twice the price at which they sold them. Islington, for instance, paid £21.6 million for 56 homes it had previously sold for £9.4 million. In November 2024, the government sharply reduced the incentive to buy, capping the discount at £16,000 in most of London.

The rulebook · Right to Buy since 1980; since November 2024 the discount is at most £16,000 in most of London · since 2024 councils keep all the money from sales · a £39bn national programme for 2026–36 pays councils and housing associations to build social and affordable homes · no specific target for council homes.

Rome

Rome has more public housing than any other Italian city, yet very little of it becomes available to new tenants. The regional housing agency, ATER, and the city together own 72,327 flats. In 2019 these accounted for 5.7 per cent of Rome's housing stock, roughly twice the national average of 2.6 per cent. But turnover is slow, and few homes become available for new tenants. In 2024 the city allocated just 208 flats to households on its waiting list, around 85 of them owned by ATER. Some 18,000 households are waiting. At that rate, clearing the list would take about 90 years.

Even the existing stock is not fully usable. In early 2025, another 309 ATER flats were unavailable for letting because they were awaiting technical inspections or had been seized by the authorities. The city itself had just 40 vacant flats, only six of which were earmarked for applicants on the waiting list.

Much of Rome's public housing is concentrated in large estates built when Italy still financed housing through a levy on workers' wages. The five largest concentrations — Torre Angela, San Basilio, Tufello, Ostia Nord and Primavalle — account for nearly 30 per cent of the stock; the twenty largest hold around two-thirds. That funding mechanism was abolished in 1998 and never replaced. Since then, sales have further eroded the public stock: in 2001, public housing accounted for about 6.7 per cent of all homes in Rome, compared with 5.7 per cent in 2019.

The rulebook · Public homes go to households below an income limit set by the Lazio region · the homes are split between the region (ATER Roma) and the city · no national building programme since the GESCAL wage levy ended in 1998 · recovery-plan money from the EU and Italy pays to renovate homes, not to build new ones.

03 · Comparison

The six cities compared

The chart below compares how much of each city's housing was social at the start of its records and today. Vienna has kept its share for more than thirty years. Helsinki is back where it was in 1990: its share rose as the state funded new building in the 1990s, then fell after 2005 as the city grew. Paris has gone up, because the law requires it. Three cities have gone down: Rome slightly, London by more than a quarter and Berlin by four-fifths, from about one home in five to one in twenty-four. This is where the EU's first Affordable Housing Plan, published in December 2025, starts from.

04 · Methodology

Sources & methodology

Every number on this page comes from a public source, listed below by city. Where we put a series together ourselves, we say so. Each country defines social housing in its own way.

Countries
Shares come from the OECD Affordable Housing Database (indicator PH4.2, updated November 2025), read from its country data file. Each is a country's latest figure, from a year between 2013 and 2022: hover over a country to see which. The UK figure covers England only. Sweden is left out because its city-owned homes are let at negotiated rents, not below market. Greece has no social rental housing in the OECD data: its workers' housing agency was abolished in 2012. Europe's 18 million is the European part of the OECD's 28 million. In the chart, the countries of the six cities are in their city's colour; in the "Out of 100 homes" view, each square is one home in a hundred.
City maps
Each map shows social homes per km², by district or, in the heatmap, smoothed over about 500 m. The colour scale is the same in every city, and the 5 km bar shows how big each city is. Vienna and Paris publish every social building: Vienna's estate register (2019) and France's RPLS (2025). London, Helsinki and Rome publish counts by neighbourhood or district: the 2021 Census, the city's Aluesarjat database and, for Rome, mapparoma's compilation of city and ATER records, which warns that they contain errors. Berlin's register lists only the plots of land the city's housing companies own homes on, without flat counts, so its map shows the share of each district's land they hold. Along a city's edge the heatmap is slightly understated. Each map's caption gives the counts behind it.

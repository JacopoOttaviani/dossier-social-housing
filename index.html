The Homes Europe Stopped Building

Europe has about 18 million social homes. The Netherlands rents out a third of its housing below market; Italy, about one home in forty. Between those two figures sit forty years of sales, expiring subsidies and waiting lists that now run to millions of households. This dossier covers six cities and what happened to their public housing.

November 2026 · Data: OECD, Housing Europe, Eurostat, municipal registers · ~9 min read

Photo: Le Corbusier's Unité d'Habitation, Marseille — Claudia Salini / Unsplash

Most large European cities keep a waiting list for social housing. London's holds 341,421 households, its longest since 2013. Paris has 327,000 applicant households; France as a whole had 2.8 million in 2024. Amsterdam reports a waiting time rather than a number: about ten years before a tenant gets a key. Vienna is the main exception: by the city's count about half its population lives in social or subsidised housing, and it approved 4,054 new subsidised homes in 2025. Finland is the other. Until 2024 it was the only European country where homelessness had fallen over a long period, and Helsinki gives homeless people a permanent flat before setting any other condition.

Social housing means homes rented below the market and allocated by need rather than by price. The OECD counts close to 28 million such dwellings across its member countries, about 18 million of them in Europe, and puts the EU average at 8 per cent of the housing stock. Few countries are close to that average. In the Netherlands the sector is 34 per cent of all homes; in Austria 24, in Denmark 21, in England 16, in France 14. Below France the shares drop sharply: Germany, Hungary and Slovakia under 3 per cent, Italy 2.4, Spain and Portugal about 1. Greece has no public rental sector at all; its workers' housing body was abolished in 2012. The OECD's most recent figures show that over the past decade the sector shrank in 18 of 25 member countries, and in 15 of the 18 European countries with figures for both years.

Most of that decline was the result of policy. England sold about 1.9 million council homes to their tenants at a discount. London's councils sold 318,909 of them, close to the 341,421 households now on the city's waiting lists. Berlin sold more than 200,000 municipal flats between 1990 and the mid-2000s, 65,700 of them in a single 2004 deal with Cerberus and Goldman Sachs; Dresden sold its entire stock. From 2013 to 2022 the Netherlands levied a tax on landlords, paid mostly by its housing associations, that rose to €1.7 billion a year. In Germany the subsidies themselves expire: after 15 to 30 years a Sozialwohnung becomes an ordinary flat. In 2025, 8,387 Berlin flats left the system that way, while the city approved subsidies for 5,175 new ones.

Keeping the stock did not shield any city from the rent crisis, but it kept a large share of households out of the open market: in Vienna, 42 per cent of households rent from the city or a limited-profit landlord. Researchers have called the stability of social housing in Vienna and Helsinki "remarkable". Where the stock was sold, public spending has risen instead. London councils are buying back homes sold under Right to Buy, Islington at more than twice the price it sold them for, and London houses 76,020 households in temporary accommodation.

The European Union addressed the issue only recently. In December 2025 the Commission presented its first Affordable Housing Plan, under its first Housing Commissioner, with an EIB investment platform behind it. On 9 September 2026 it proposed an Affordable Housing Act, now before Parliament and Council, which sets common rules for local housing measures, including when cities may restrict short-term rentals in areas under housing stress. This dossier sets out the position those measures start from. The same point recurs across the six cities: the sectors that held or grew their share had a lasting commitment behind them, a payroll levy in Vienna and a legal quota in Paris, and both London and Berlin now pay far more per home to buy housing back than they received when they sold.

01 · The map

From 34 per cent in the Netherlands to 2.4 in Italy

The five countries with the largest sectors (the Netherlands, Austria, Denmark, the United Kingdom and France) all maintained one through the 1990s and 2000s. In southern Europe and in the former Eastern bloc, social housing was either never built at that scale or sold to its tenants after 1990. The gap between the highest and lowest shares is more than fortyfold.

02 · Six cities

City by city

Vienna kept building. Helsinki used its stock to house homeless people, even as that stock shrank as a share of a growing city. Paris raised its share because a national law requires it. Berlin and London lost theirs, Berlin through sales and expiring subsidies, London through sales. Rome still owns most of its stock but allocates very few flats. Each city below has a map, a chart and its main figures.

Vienna Helsinki Paris Berlin London Rome

Vienna

Vienna did not have to rebuild its social housing because it never sold its municipal stock. The city owns about 220,000 flats and calls itself Europe's largest municipal landlord; limited-profit associations hold about 185,000 more. Together they house 42 per cent of the city's households. Income limits are set high: by the city's count, three-quarters of Viennese earn less than the limit. Completions of subsidised homes have run at about 3,000 to 4,500 a year; approvals fell to about 2,000 in 2022 and recovered to 4,054 in 2025. Part of the money comes from a payroll levy created in 1952 and set at 1 per cent of wages since 1954; in January 2026 Vienna, alone among the Länder, raised it to 1.5 per cent. Red Vienna had built some 64,000 flats between 1923 and 1934 with an earmarked municipal housing tax, which was abolished in the 1930s; today's levy is a separate, later contribution.

The rulebook · Municipal ownership since the 1920s (Rotes Wien) · limited-profit sector regulated by federal law (WGG), with a purchase option for some tenants · payroll housing levy since 1952, at 1% of wages since 1954, raised to 1.5% in Jan 2026 · no sales of municipal flats, no expiring subsidies.

Helsinki

Helsinki owns about 65 per cent of the land inside its boundaries and steers what is built on it through land sales, leases and tenure targets. Its housing company Heka is Finland's largest landlord, with about 55,000 flats, home to one Helsinki resident in seven. Since 2008 that stock has supported the national Housing First policy: a homeless person is given a permanent flat with no preconditions, and support services follow. For years Finland was the only EU country where homelessness kept falling, from about 17,100 people living alone in 1987 to 3,429 in 2023. The government elected in 2023 kept the programme's own budget flat but cut housing allowance and social assistance, halved state funding for housing advice and reduced grants to the charities that provide much of the support. The count rose in 2024 for the first time since 2012, and by a further fifth in 2025. The subsidised stock has held at about 65,000 to 70,000 flats for two decades, but as the city grew its share of homes fell from 22 per cent in 2005 to 16 per cent, partly because older flats have been released from the arava rules.

The rulebook · City owns about 65% of its land and leases much of it, with tenure targets for new building · ARA interest-subsidy loans fund non-profit building · Housing First national programmes since 2008 · shelters converted to flats · benefit, housing-advice and charity funding cut from 2024.

Paris

Paris is the only city in this dossier whose social share has risen substantially, and it rose because a law requires it. The 2000 SRU act set a quota for French communes above a minimum size, 20 per cent of main residences and 25 per cent since 2013, with financial penalties for communes that fall short. Paris has financed 130,316 social homes since 2001 and moved from 13.4 to 23.2 per cent, buying existing buildings where it cannot build new ones: purchases supplied more than half of the homes financed since 2001. The waiting list has grown anyway: 327,000 applicant households at the end of 2025, just under half of them already living in Paris, for a stock that turns over slowly in a city where average private rents, at €26.3 a square metre, are about three times the average social rent. France's national list of 2.8 million households in 2024 is the largest in Europe in absolute terms.

The rulebook · SRU law (2000): 20% social, raised to 25% in 2013; the 2025 deadline replaced by a permanent catch-up rule (3DS law, 2022) · penalties for non-compliant communes · HLM rent ceilings set nationally by loan type and zone · private rent control reinstated in July 2019.

Berlin

Berlin had 85,765 social-rent flats at the end of 2024, down from 370,861 in 1991, and it has lost them in two ways. German subsidies expire: after 15 to 30 years a Sozialwohnung becomes a market flat, so the stock shrinks even without any sale. In 2025, 8,387 flats left the subsidy system, while the city approved funding for 5,175 new ones, most of which will take years to build. Berlin also privatised more than 200,000 municipal flats between 1990 and the mid-2000s. The largest deal was the whole GSW company: 65,700 flats sold in 2004 to Cerberus and Goldman Sachs' Whitehall fund for €405 million plus about €1.5 billion of debt, roughly €30,000 a flat. The funds floated GSW in April 2011 and had sold their last shares by January 2012; in 2013 Deutsche Wohnen took over the listed company for about €1.8 billion in shares. In 2021 the city paid €2.46 billion, about €167,000 a flat, to buy 14,750 flats from Vonovia and Deutsche Wohnen. In September 2021, 57.6 per cent of voters backed expropriating the largest landlords, about 243,000 flats on the Senate's estimate. The vote asked the Senate to draft a law but did not bind it. In March 2026 the city passed a framework law on socialisation that takes effect only in 2028; no flats have been transferred under it. In July 2026 the federal coalition said it would bar state laws that socialise rental housing, and on 20 September Die Linke, which backed the 2021 campaign, came first in the Berlin election with 25.7 per cent.

The rulebook · Time-limited subsidies (federal model) · privatisations from 1990 to the mid-2000s, partly reversed by re-purchases · 2021 expropriation referendum passed, still unimplemented · socialisation framework law passed March 2026, in force from 2028 · state-owned companies rebuilding stock.

London

Right to Buy, introduced in 1980, let council tenants buy their homes at a discount, at first of up to 50 per cent and later of up to 70. London's councils sold 318,909 homes this way by March 2025, close to the 341,421 households on the city's waiting lists. Council stock in London fell from a peak of 795,789 in 1986 to 389,819 in 2021, through these sales and through transfers of estates to housing associations. Counting housing associations, social housing fell from 29 to 21 per cent of London's dwellings between 1991 and 2025. The council stock has barely grown since 2021, reaching 394,591 in 2025. London now houses 76,020 households in temporary accommodation, at a cost London Councils puts at almost £5 million a day, and some boroughs are buying back former council homes at more than twice the price they sold them for: Islington paid £21.6 million for 56 homes it had sold for £9.4 million. Discounts were cut in November 2024, to a maximum of £16,000 in most of London.

The rulebook · Right to Buy since 1980, discounts capped at £16,000 in most of London since November 2024 · councils keep 100% of sale receipts since 2024 · the £39bn Social and Affordable Homes Programme 2026–36 funds councils and housing associations · no target specific to council homes.

Rome

Rome has more public housing than any other Italian city and allocates very little of it. The 72,327 flats held by ATER, the regional agency, and the municipality were 5.7 per cent of the city's dwellings in 2019, about twice the Italian average of 2.6 per cent. Access is limited by the rate of reallocation rather than by the size of the stock. In 2024 the city assigned 208 flats from its waiting list, about 85 of them released by ATER, against roughly 18,000 households on it. At that rate, clearing the current list would take about 90 years. In early 2025 a further 309 ATER flats were unavailable, awaiting technical inspection or under seizure, and the city itself had only 40 vacant flats, six of them earmarked for the list. The stock is concentrated in large estates built when Italy still collected a national housing levy: the five biggest districts, Torre Angela, San Basilio, Tufello, Ostia Nord and Primavalle, hold nearly 30 per cent of it, and the twenty biggest two-thirds. That levy ended in 1998 and was not replaced, and sales have since shrunk the stock: it was about 6.7 per cent of Rome's dwellings in 2001.

The rulebook · ERP assigned under Lazio regional law on a household income test · stock split between the region (ATER Roma) and the city · no national construction programme since the GESCAL levy ended in 1998 · PNRR and complementary-fund money pays for renovation, not new building.

03 · Comparison

The six cities compared

The chart below shows the social share of each city's homes at its earliest published measure and at the latest. Vienna has held its share for more than thirty years. Helsinki is back where it was in 1990: its share rose with new state-subsidised building in the 1990s and has fallen since 2005 as the city grew. Paris rises, under the SRU law. Three fall: Rome by one percentage point, London by eight, Berlin by seventeen. The EU's first Affordable Housing Plan, published in December 2025, starts from this position.

Vienna +1.1

Kept its stock. Continuous municipal ownership since the 1920s, a payroll levy that has never lapsed, and a limited-profit sector that kept adding homes. Income limits are high enough that most Viennese qualify, so the sector is not restricted to the lowest incomes. Approvals of new subsidised homes dipped to about 2,000 in 2022 and recovered to 4,054 in 2025.

Paris +9.8

Grew under a legal quota. The SRU law made a minimum share of social housing a legal requirement rather than a policy choice: 130,316 homes financed since 2001, and a rise from 13.4% to 23.2% of main residences. The waiting list still stands at 327,000 households.

Helsinki −0.2

Used its stock for Housing First. City-owned land, a subsidised stock of 65,000 to 70,000 flats, and a policy in place since 2008 of giving homeless people a permanent flat with no preconditions. Homelessness in Finland fell by 80% between 1987 and 2023. The subsidised share of homes, 22% in 2005, is back to 16% as the city grows, and after cuts to benefits and support services the 2025 homelessness count rose by a fifth.

Amsterdam −21

Taxed its housing associations. The Netherlands' social sector, proportionally Europe's largest, was squeezed by two decisions: an EU state-aid decision capped eligibility by income, and a national levy took up to €1.7bn a year from landlords, most of it from the associations that build. The levy was scrapped in 2023; the income cap still applies. The average wait is now about ten years.

London −8.2

Sold its stock, now buys some back. Right to Buy moved 318,909 London council homes into private ownership, close to the 341,421 households now on the city's waiting lists. Islington has paid more than twice the sale price to buy former council homes back, and London houses 76,020 households in temporary accommodation.

Berlin −17.4

Sold its stock, then voted to reverse it. More than 200,000 flats privatised between 1990 and the mid-2000s, and subsidies expiring faster than new ones are granted. In 2021 a majority voted to expropriate the largest landlords; a framework law followed in March 2026 and takes effect in 2028. No flats have been transferred.

Rome −1

Kept most of the stock, stopped allocating it. Italy stopped funding public housing when the GESCAL levy ended in 1998 and did not replace it. Rome's stock is large: 72,327 flats, 5.7% of the city's dwellings in 2019, down from about 6.7% in 2001. In 2024, 208 flats were assigned from a list of about 18,000 households, while across Italy 61,300 public flats stand empty awaiting repair.

04 · Methodology

Sources & methodology

Every number on this page is traceable to a public source. Where we compiled a series ourselves, we say so and link the underlying publisher. "Social housing" is defined differently in each country, so each definition is stated here.

Country shares & the map
OECD Affordable Housing Database, indicator PH4.2 — Social rental housing stock (updated 24 Nov 2025): social rental dwellings as a share of total dwellings, 2022 or latest year, from the OECD QuASH questionnaire. Every country value on the map is read from the indicator's country data file, not from the published chart, and carries its own reference year (2013–2022), which limits comparability, so the year is shown on hover. Three caveats apply: the OECD's "United Kingdom" covers England only; Sweden is excluded by definition, because municipal rents there are negotiated rather than sub-market; and Greece is absent from the dataset: the OECD lists it among the countries with no social rental housing (indicator PH4.3), and its workers' housing body, the OEK, was abolished in 2012. The European total of about 18 million is the sum of the European countries in the OECD's table of dwelling numbers; its 28 million also covers the US, Japan, Korea and other non-European members.
How the map is built
Each country is plotted at its capital and named on the map with its share beside the name. Circle area is proportional to the share; the radius scales with its square root. Names are placed by trying eight positions around each mark and taking the first that clears the labels already placed; on narrow screens the type grows to stay legible and names that no longer fit are dropped. The table beneath lists every country.
The city cluster maps
One map per city, each drawn to the same frame and the same circle-size scale, so a 1,000-home estate is the same size everywhere. The cities differ greatly in extent, so the 5 km bar on each map is what makes them comparable. What a circle represents differs by city, because the underlying registers differ. Two countries publish social housing at building level and we map the buildings: Vienna's Gemeindebau map layer (last updated 2019: 1,657 complexes, 210,512 flats; the city repeats each complex's flat count on every building of the complex, so we count it once per address; Wiener Wohnen's current register lists 1,690 complexes and about 218,600 flats) and France's RPLS, a register of every social rental home, geolocated (9,645 Paris addresses, 248,447 homes; 2,193 dwellings carry no coordinates and are not drawn). Three do not, so the circles sit on neighbourhood centroids and those maps show density by neighbourhood rather than individual estates: London from the 2021 Census, table TS054, social-rented households per LSOA (4,994 neighbourhoods, 4,993 of them with social renters, 790,862 households); Helsinki from Aluesarjat, arava and interest-subsidised rentals per district at 31 December 2024 (65,088 flats, 16.3% of the city's 398,541 dwellings, or 19.7% with right-of-occupancy flats); and Rome from mapparoma's compilation of Roma Capitale and ATER records for 2019, geocoded from addresses (72,327 homes across 131 zone urbanistiche). Neither the city nor the region publishes a register of its own, and the compilation warns that the databases behind it contain inaccuracies. Berlin is drawn differently: its register maps the parcels its state-owned housing companies hold housing on and attaches no dwelling figures at all, so there is nothing to size a circle by; the map shows the land itself, and it counts municipal-company stock rather than the shrinking Sozialwohnungen category. Boundaries come from each city's own register (ONS for London); rivers and coastlines from those registers and from OpenStreetMap.
How the city heatmaps are drawn
Each city map opens on the circles and switches to a heatmap, and both draw on the same records. The heatmap spreads each record's homes over its surroundings with a Gaussian kernel (a standard deviation of 500 m), then sorts the result into six bands of homes per km²: 100–250, 250–500, 500–1,000, 1,000–2,000, 2,000–4,000, and 4,000 or more. Land under 100 homes per km² is left blank. The bands are the same in every city, so a colour means the same density in Vienna as in Paris. Where the register gives one count per area rather than per building (Helsinki's districts, Rome's zone urbanistiche), each area's homes are first spread evenly across the area and then smoothed, so the map shows density by area rather than inventing estates inside it. London's neighbourhoods are small enough to be treated as points. Berlin's register carries no dwelling counts, so its heatmap measures something else: the share of the ground covered by the companies' parcels, smoothed the same way and banded at 2, 5, 8, 12, 16 and 20 per cent or more. Near a city's boundary, part of the smoothing falls outside the city and is cut off, so the colour along the edge is slightly understated.

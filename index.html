The Homes Europe Stopped Building

Europe has about 18 million social homes, rented below market price to people who need them. In the Netherlands they make up a third of all housing; in Italy, about one home in forty. Over the past forty years many cities sold theirs or let the subsidies run out, and waiting lists now run to millions of households. This dossier looks at six cities and what happened to their public housing.

November 2026 · Data: OECD, Housing Europe, Eurostat, municipal registers · ~10 min read

Photo: Le Corbusier's Unité d'Habitation, Marseille — Claudia Salini / Unsplash

Most big European cities have a waiting list for social housing. In London, 341,421 households are on it, the most since 2013. In Paris there are 327,000, and across France 2.8 million in 2024. Amsterdam measures the wait in time: about ten years before a tenant gets a key. Vienna is the main exception. By the city's count, about half of its people live in social or subsidised housing, and in 2025 it approved 4,054 new subsidised homes. Finland is the other exception. Until 2024 it was the only European country where homelessness had gone down over the long term. Helsinki gives homeless people a permanent flat straight away, with no conditions to meet first.

Social housing means homes rented below market price and given out according to need, not to whoever can pay most. The OECD, an organisation of mostly wealthy countries, counts close to 28 million such homes among its members, about 18 million of them in Europe. On average they are 8 per cent of all homes in the EU, but few countries are close to that average. In the Netherlands social housing is 34 per cent of all homes; in Austria 24, in Denmark 21, in England 16 and in France 14. After that the numbers drop sharply: under 3 per cent in Germany, Hungary and Slovakia, 2.4 in Italy, about 1 in Spain and Portugal. Greece has none at all: its workers' housing agency was shut down in 2012. According to the OECD's latest figures, social housing shrank over the past decade in 18 of its 25 member countries. In Europe it shrank in 15 of the 18 countries with figures for both years.

Most of that decline was a political choice. Cities and governments sold their social homes, often to the tenants at a discount, or let the subsidies run out.

Keeping social housing did not protect any city from rising rents. But it kept many households out of the private market. In Vienna, 42 per cent of households rent from the city or from limited-profit landlords, companies that by law must put their profits back into housing. Researchers have called the stability of social housing in Vienna and Helsinki "remarkable". Where cities sold their homes, they now spend more public money instead. London councils are buying back homes sold to tenants under Right to Buy, Islington at more than twice the price it sold them for. And 76,020 London households live in temporary accommodation, such as B&Bs and hostels.

The European Union has taken up the issue only recently. In December 2025 the European Commission presented its first Affordable Housing Plan, under its first commissioner for housing, backed by an investment platform at the European Investment Bank. On 9 September 2026 it proposed an Affordable Housing Act, now before the European Parliament and EU governments. It would set common rules for local housing measures, including when cities can limit short-term holiday rentals in areas short of homes. This dossier shows where those measures start from. The six cities point the same way. Where social housing held or grew, a lasting commitment stood behind it: a levy on wages in Vienna, a legal quota in Paris. And London and Berlin now pay far more per home to buy housing back than they got when they sold it.

Country figures come from the OECD's housing database; each refers to a year between 2013 and 2022. The Cormoran compiled the city figures from each city's statistics office or housing agency, and every one is a published figure. Each city defines "social housing" in its own way. 01 · Europe

From 34 per cent in the Netherlands to 2.4 in Italy

The five countries with the most social housing (the Netherlands, Austria, Denmark, the United Kingdom and France) all kept a large sector going through the 1990s and 2000s. In southern Europe and in the former communist countries of the East, it was either never built on that scale or was sold to its tenants after 1990. The highest share is more than forty times the lowest.

02 · Six cities

City by city

Vienna kept building. Helsinki used its social homes to house homeless people, even though they became a smaller part of a growing city. Paris raised its share because a national law requires it. Berlin and London lost much of theirs: Berlin by selling flats and letting subsidies run out, London by selling. Rome still owns most of its homes but hands out very few. Each city below has a map and its key figures.

Vienna Helsinki Paris Berlin London Rome

Vienna

Vienna never sold its council flats, so it never had to rebuild them. The city owns about 220,000 flats and calls itself Europe's largest municipal landlord. Limited-profit associations, which by law must put their profits back into housing, own about 185,000 more. Together they house 42 per cent of the city's households. The income limits are high, so social housing is not only for the poor: by the city's count, three-quarters of Viennese earn less than the limit. Usually about 3,000 to 4,500 subsidised homes are finished each year. Approvals for new ones fell to about 2,000 in 2022 and rose again to 4,054 in 2025. Part of the money comes from a small levy on wages, 1 per cent since 1954. In January 2026 Vienna raised it to 1.5 per cent, the only Austrian state to do so. Between 1923 and 1934 the city's socialist government, known as Red Vienna, had already built some 64,000 flats with a special housing tax. That tax was abolished in the 1930s; today's wage levy is a separate one, introduced later.

The rulebook · The city has owned housing since the 1920s (Red Vienna) · limited-profit landlords are regulated by a federal law, and some tenants can buy their flat · a housing levy on wages since 1952: 1% since 1954, 1.5% since January 2026 · the city does not sell its flats, and its subsidies do not expire.

Helsinki

Helsinki owns about 65 per cent of the land within its borders. By selling and leasing it, and by setting targets for the mix of new homes, the city decides what gets built. Its housing company, Heka, is Finland's largest landlord, with about 55,000 flats: one Helsinki resident in seven lives in one. Since 2008 these flats have served Finland's Housing First policy. A homeless person gets a permanent flat with no conditions attached, and help with other problems comes afterwards. For years Finland was the only EU country where homelessness kept falling: from about 17,100 people living alone in 1987 to 3,429 in 2023. The government elected in 2023 did not cut the programme itself. But it cut housing benefits and welfare payments, halved state money for housing advice and gave less to the charities that provide much of the support. Homelessness rose in 2024 for the first time since 2012, and by another fifth in 2025. The number of subsidised flats has stayed at about 65,000 to 70,000 for twenty years. But the city kept growing, so their share of all homes fell from 22 per cent in 2005 to 16 per cent. Another reason is that older flats have come out of the subsidy rules over time.

The rulebook · The city owns about 65% of its land and leases much of it, with targets for the mix of new homes · cheap state-backed loans fund non-profit building · national Housing First programmes since 2008 · homeless shelters turned into flats · cuts to benefits, housing advice and charity funding from 2024.

Paris

Paris is the only city in this dossier where the share of social housing has risen a lot, and it rose because the law says it must. A 2000 law known as SRU requires French towns above a certain size to make social housing at least 20 per cent of their main homes. In 2013 the bar was raised to 25 per cent. Towns that fall short pay fines. Since 2001 Paris has funded 130,316 social homes and raised its share from 13.4 to 23.2 per cent. Where it cannot build, it buys existing buildings: more than half of the homes funded since 2001 were bought, not built. The waiting list has grown anyway, to 327,000 households at the end of 2025. Just under half of them already live in Paris. Social homes rarely come free. Private rents in Paris average €26.3 a square metre, about three times the average social rent. France as a whole had 2.8 million households waiting in 2024, the most of any country in Europe.

The rulebook · SRU law (2000): 20% social housing, raised to 25% in 2013; in 2022 the 2025 deadline was replaced by a rule that makes towns keep catching up · fines for towns that fall short · social rents capped nationally, depending on the area and how the home was funded · private rents capped again since July 2019.

Berlin

Berlin had 85,765 social-rent flats at the end of 2024, down from 370,861 in 1991. It lost them in two ways. First, German subsidies expire. After 15 to 30 years a social flat becomes an ordinary market flat, so the number shrinks even if nothing is sold. In 2025, 8,387 flats left the subsidy system, while the city approved funding for 5,175 new ones, most of which will take years to build. Second, Berlin sold more than 200,000 city-owned flats between 1990 and the mid-2000s. The biggest sale was a whole housing company, GSW. Its 65,700 flats went in 2004 to investment funds run by Cerberus and Goldman Sachs, for roughly €30,000 a flat, including the debt the buyers took on. The funds put GSW on the stock market in 2011 and had sold all their shares by January 2012. In 2013 the property company Deutsche Wohnen took it over. In 2021 the city went the other way. It paid €2.46 billion, about €167,000 a flat, to buy 14,750 flats from Vonovia and Deutsche Wohnen. That September, 57.6 per cent of voters backed taking the biggest landlords' flats into public ownership: about 243,000 homes, by the city government's estimate. The vote asked the city government to draft a law but did not force it to. In March 2026 the city passed a law setting out how such a takeover could work. But it takes effect only in 2028, and no flats have changed hands. In July 2026 the federal government said it would ban state laws that take rental housing into public ownership. On 20 September Die Linke, the left-wing party that backed the 2021 campaign, won the most votes in the Berlin election, with 25.7 per cent.

The rulebook · Subsidies that expire after a set time (the German model) · city flats sold from 1990 to the mid-2000s, some since bought back · 2021 referendum on taking big landlords' flats into public ownership: passed, not yet carried out · law on how to do it passed March 2026, in force from 2028 · the city's own housing companies are adding homes again.

London

Right to Buy, a policy introduced in 1980, lets council tenants buy their homes at a discount: at first up to 50 per cent off, later up to 70. By March 2025 London's councils had sold 318,909 homes this way, almost as many as the 341,421 households now on the city's waiting lists. The number of council homes in London fell from a peak of 795,789 in 1986 to 389,819 in 2021. Some were sold; others were handed to housing associations, non-profit landlords that still rent them out as social housing. Counting both, social housing fell from 29 to 21 per cent of London's homes between 1991 and 2025. Since 2021 the number of council homes has barely grown, reaching 394,591 in 2025. Meanwhile 76,020 households live in temporary accommodation, such as B&Bs and hostels. London Councils, the boroughs' association, puts the cost at almost £5 million a day. Some boroughs are buying former council homes back for more than twice what they sold them for. Islington paid £21.6 million for 56 homes it had sold for £9.4 million. In November 2024 the discount was cut to at most £16,000 in most of London.

The rulebook · Right to Buy since 1980; since November 2024 the discount is at most £16,000 in most of London · since 2024 councils keep all the money from sales · a £39bn national programme for 2026–36 pays councils and housing associations to build social and affordable homes · no specific target for council homes.

Rome

Rome has more public housing than any other Italian city, but hands out very little of it. The regional housing agency, ATER, and the city own 72,327 flats between them. In 2019 that was 5.7 per cent of Rome's homes, about twice the Italian average of 2.6 per cent. Few of these homes ever come free for new tenants. In 2024 the city gave 208 flats to households on its waiting list, about 85 of them from ATER. Roughly 18,000 households are on the list. At that pace it would take about 90 years to house them all. In early 2025 another 309 ATER flats could not be let, because they were waiting for technical checks or had been seized by the authorities. The city itself had only 40 empty flats, six of them set aside for the list. Most of the homes are in large estates built when Italy still had a national levy on wages to pay for housing. The five biggest neighbourhoods, Torre Angela, San Basilio, Tufello, Ostia Nord and Primavalle, hold nearly 30 per cent of them, and the twenty biggest hold two-thirds. That levy ended in 1998 and nothing replaced it. Since then, sales have shrunk the stock: in 2001 it was about 6.7 per cent of Rome's homes.

The rulebook · Public homes go to households below an income limit set by the Lazio region · the homes are split between the region (ATER Roma) and the city · no national building programme since the GESCAL wage levy ended in 1998 · recovery-plan money from the EU and Italy pays to renovate homes, not to build new ones.

03 · Comparison

The six cities compared

The chart below compares how much of each city's housing was social at the start of its records and today. Vienna has kept its share for more than thirty years. Helsinki is back where it was in 1990: its share rose as the state funded new building in the 1990s, then fell after 2005 as the city grew. Paris has gone up, because the law requires it. Three cities have gone down: Rome slightly, London by more than a quarter and Berlin by four-fifths, from about one home in five to one in twenty-four. This is where the EU's first Affordable Housing Plan, published in December 2025, starts from.

Vienna 40.9% → 42%

Kept its homes. The city has owned housing since the 1920s, a levy on wages has paid for new homes without a break since 1952, and its limited-profit landlords kept building. Income limits are high enough that most Viennese qualify, so social housing is not only for the poorest. Approvals for new subsidised homes dipped to about 2,000 in 2022 and recovered to 4,054 in 2025.

Paris 13.4% → 23.2%

Grew because the law requires it. The SRU law made a minimum share of social housing compulsory rather than optional. Since 2001 Paris has funded 130,316 social homes, and their share of main homes has risen from 13.4% to 23.2%. The waiting list is still 327,000 households long.

Helsinki 16.5% → 16.3%

Used its homes for Housing First. The city owns most of its land and 65,000 to 70,000 subsidised flats. Since 2008 homeless people have been given a permanent flat with no conditions attached. Homelessness in Finland fell by 80% between 1987 and 2023. As the city grew, the subsidised share of homes went from 22% in 2005 back to 16%. After cuts to benefits and support services, homelessness rose by a fifth in 2025.

Amsterdam 58% → 37%

Taxed its housing associations. For its size, the Netherlands has the biggest social housing sector in Europe. Two decisions squeezed it. An EU ruling on state aid limited social homes to people below an income limit. And a national tax took up to €1.7bn a year from landlords, mostly from the non-profit housing associations that build. The tax was scrapped in 2023; the income limit still applies. The average wait is now about ten years.

London 29.2% → 21%

Sold its homes, now buys some back. Under Right to Buy, 318,909 London council homes were sold to their tenants, almost as many as the 341,421 households now on the city's waiting lists. Islington has paid more than twice the sale price to buy some back, and 76,020 London households live in temporary accommodation.

Berlin 21.6% → 4.2%

Sold its homes, then voted to undo it. More than 200,000 city flats were sold between 1990 and the mid-2000s, and subsidies run out faster than new ones are granted. In 2021 a majority voted to take the biggest landlords' flats into public ownership. A law on how to do it followed in March 2026 and takes effect in 2028. No flats have changed hands.

Rome 6.7% → 5.7%

Kept most of its homes, stopped handing them out. Italy stopped funding public housing when its national wage levy, GESCAL, ended in 1998, and nothing replaced it. Rome still has a lot: 72,327 flats, 5.7% of its homes in 2019, down from about 6.7% in 2001. In 2024 it gave out 208 flats to a list of about 18,000 households. Across Italy, 61,300 public flats stand empty, waiting for repairs.

04 · Methodology

Sources & methodology

Every number on this page comes from a public source, listed below. Where we put a series together ourselves, we say so and link the original publisher. Each country defines "social housing" differently, so each definition is given here.

Country shares & the chart
OECD Affordable Housing Database, indicator PH4.2 — Social rental housing stock (updated 24 Nov 2025): social rental dwellings as a share of total dwellings, 2022 or latest year, from the OECD QuASH questionnaire. Every country value in the chart is read from the indicator's country data file, not from the published chart, and carries its own reference year (2013–2022), which limits comparability, so the year is shown on hover. Three caveats apply: the OECD's "United Kingdom" covers England only; Sweden is excluded by definition, because municipal rents there are negotiated rather than sub-market; and Greece is absent from the dataset: the OECD lists it among the countries with no social rental housing (indicator PH4.3), and its workers' housing body, the OEK, was abolished in 2012. The European total of about 18 million is the sum of the European countries in the OECD's table of dwelling numbers; its 28 million also covers the US, Japan, Korea and other non-European members.
How the chart is built
The chart has three views of the same figures, one switch apart. Bars: each country's share on one scale, longest first, with the EU average quoted in the introduction marked across them. Out of 100 homes: a grid of 100 squares for each country, filled from the bottom up with its share; the last square is filled in part, so 34.1% is 34 squares and a tenth. By region: each country's share as a dot on one scale, grouped as the text groups them: northern and western Europe, southern Europe, and the formerly communist centre and east. The countries of the six cities in this dossier are in their city's colour; Sweden and Greece, which have no figure, are marked ✳. Hovering over a country shows the year of its figure and the OECD's note. The table beneath lists every country.
The city cluster maps
One map per city, each drawn to the same frame and coloured on the same scale, so a colour means the same density everywhere. The cities differ greatly in extent, so the 5 km bar on each map is what makes them comparable. What each map starts from differs by city, because the underlying registers differ. Two countries publish social housing at building level, and we count the buildings into their districts: Vienna's Gemeindebau map layer (last updated 2019: 1,657 complexes, 210,512 flats; the city repeats each complex's flat count on every building of the complex, so we count it once per address; Wiener Wohnen's current register lists 1,690 complexes and about 218,600 flats) and France's RPLS, a register of every social rental home, geolocated (9,645 Paris addresses, 248,447 homes; 2,193 dwellings carry no coordinates and are not drawn). Three do not, so those maps start from a count for each neighbourhood or district rather than from individual estates: London from the 2021 Census, table TS054, social-rented households per LSOA (4,994 neighbourhoods, 4,993 of them with social renters, 790,862 households); Helsinki from Aluesarjat, arava and interest-subsidised rentals per district at 31 December 2024 (65,088 flats, 16.3% of the city's 398,541 dwellings, or 19.7% with right-of-occupancy flats); and Rome from mapparoma's compilation of Roma Capitale and ATER records for 2019, geocoded from addresses (72,327 homes across 131 zone urbanistiche). Neither the city nor the region publishes a register of its own, and the compilation warns that the databases behind it contain inaccuracies. Berlin is drawn differently: its register maps the parcels its state-owned housing companies hold housing on and attaches no dwelling figures at all, so there is nothing to count; the map shows how much of each district's land the companies hold, and it counts municipal-company stock rather than the shrinking Sozialwohnungen category. Boundaries come from each city's own register (ONS for London); rivers and coastlines from those registers and from OpenStreetMap.
How the city maps are drawn
Each city map opens on its districts and switches to a heatmap, and both draw on the same records. The district view adds up the homes in each district, using the city's own boundaries, and divides them by the district's area; London's neighbourhoods are counted in the borough where their centroid lies. Both views use the same colour bands, and a district with some homes but fewer than 100 per km² gets the palest shade. The heatmap spreads each record's homes over its surroundings with a Gaussian kernel (a standard deviation of 500 m), then sorts the result into six bands of homes per km²: 100–250, 250–500, 500–1,000, 1,000–2,000, 2,000–4,000, and 4,000 or more. In the heatmap, land under 100 homes per km² is left blank. The bands are the same in every city, so a colour means the same density in Vienna as in Paris. Where the register gives one count per area rather than per building (Helsinki's districts, Rome's zone urbanistiche), each area's homes are first spread evenly across the area and then smoothed, so the map shows density by area rather than inventing estates inside it. London's neighbourhoods are small enough to be treated as points. Berlin's register carries no dwelling counts, so both its views measure something else: the share of the ground covered by the companies' parcels, in each district and, in the heatmap, smoothed the same way, banded at 2, 5, 8, 12, 16 and 20 per cent or more. Near a city's boundary, part of the smoothing falls outside the city and is cut off, so the colour along the edge is slightly understated.
